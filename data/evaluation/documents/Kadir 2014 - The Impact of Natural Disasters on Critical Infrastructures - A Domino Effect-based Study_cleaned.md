## 1  Introduction

 Such effects cause catastrophic damage that affect not only the industrial sites themselves but also the people, the environment, and the economy. The trigger events are usually natural or technological in origin. The original hazard (risk) may lead to or cause derivative hazards of the same nature (e.g., fire followed by explosions; an earthquake followed by a tsunami) or a different nature (e.g., an underground facility blast that leads to seismic activity, or vice versa). For example, an earthquake may cause an industrial accident or an interruption to vital networks such as energy supply, transport, and telecommunications.

## 1  Introduction

 According to the mechanisms initiating the chain reaction and the combination of events (sequential, parallel, etc.), three main types of domino effect with natural and technological causes can be established (Cruz et al. 2004; Campedel et al. 2008; Milazzo et al. 2013; Salzano et al. 2013): In this paper we focus on the study of the impact of Nat-Tech events on critical infrastructure (CI). A European Programme for Critical Infrastructure Protection (CIP) was proposed in 2004-2005, and the European Commission adopted a Green Paper in 2005 (European Commission 2005). That report studied infrastructure that, if destroyed or disrupted in operation because of a trigger event (natural, technical, or organizational), could pose a threat to life, well-being, structures, and so on. Infrastructure considered to be critical includes industrial facilities that provide society with vital resources, and strategic networks such as telecommunications, transport, and electricity. In the US, the President's Commission on Critical Infrastructure Protection 1997 (cited by Rinaldi et al. 2001) defined CI as a network of independent, mostly privately owned, man-made systems and processes that function collaboratively and synergistically to produce and distribute a continuous flow of essential goods and services. This definition was extended in 2006 to cover other threats, with a meaning similar to that of Europe's (DHS 2006). To ensure the safety and sustainability of the population, CI must be protected, and the U.S. Department of Homeland Security (DHS) has considered CI protection to be part of the fight against terrorism since 2002-2003.

## 3    Critical Infrastructure, Sources of Danger, and the Propagation Processes

 CI can be defined as complex systems of components that ensure production, transport, communication, health, safety, and any other activities necessary for a society's (country's) needs. Its disruption or destruction would affect the teams working at these complexes, the surrounding structures, public health and safety, the economy, and national security. CI can be found in most of the following areas of activity (Little 2002a,b; Rahman 2005; Chen and Mark 2010): The difficulty in assessing the effects of a disaster on CI comes from the key nature of its interdependencies. These interdependencies can be physical, geographical, cyber, and logical, making it possible for initial events to combine and snowball, as Birregah et al. (2011) show. Using the existing interdependencies of a system, the propagation mechanism of a trigger event will go through preestablished or opportunistic paths (combinations of secondary events).

## 3.1  Sources of Danger Involving Critical Infrastructure

 The potential sources of danger affecting CI are varied in nature and can be triggered by different initial events. In general, they can be classified by the nature of the risk: natural, technological, or of human origin. A human factor-based risk can be unintentional, as with organizational risks, or intentional, as with risk of malice. As cascade effects can involve a combination of these risk categories, it is therefore possible to propose a nondisjunctive decomposition of the different natures of risk and to classify trigger events, as shown in Table 1.

## 3.1  Sources of Danger Involving Critical Infrastructure

 These risks can combine with each other and lead to complex events, the analysis of which requires a systematic approach that takes into account not only the systems involved and their interdependencies, but also the combination of events and the various propagation processes that occur in such systems. A range of analysis methods, including deterministic, probabilistic or even possibilistic, and qualitative/quantitative, can be used for CI assessment. Whatever the method used, it is important to note that potential sources of danger of an anthropogenic nature can be both internal and external to the system. It is therefore necessary to take into account both external elements (aircraft, vehicle, gas, etc.) and external (aggressor) and internal (including staff) individuals.

## 3.2  The Propagation Processes

 Propagation processes are related directly to both the potential sources of danger and trigger events and the intermediate and nearby environment (also called the 'field of danger'). They are defined by a physicochemical process as well as by an informational one, as the evolutionary conditions are determined by the physical (atmospheric, geological, and hydrological), material (buildings, sites, facilities, and roads), ecological (vegetation and animals), informational (detection and observation, information systems), and human (individual behavior, organization and logistics, and local demography) characteristics present.

## 3.2  The Propagation Processes

 The concept of source of danger is similar to that used in the framework of cindynics (Kervern 1994), which relates the propagation of danger from a potential source to a potential target (see Figure 3). In this framework, a source of danger will have an impact only if the field of danger is able to foster the propagation of said danger, which it might not be able to do if negative propagation conditions appear. If the field of danger does propagate the danger, it will hit the target(s) (CI, the environment, etc.). In the context of cascade effects, each damaged target is potentially a new source of danger, so the danger propagation process shown in Figure 3 can be extended and repeated.

## 3.2  The Propagation Processes

 Natural hazards are often triggers in terms of starting points; that is, they induce technological, human, or organizational failures simultaneously and at different locations that have no direct physical or functional relationship. Indeed, the vulnerability of an infrastructure may vary depending on the makeup or local impact of some natural phenomena (for atmospheric or geological reasons) as well as on the social or geopolitical context (see the field of danger in Figure 3).

## 3.2  The Propagation Processes

 Processes of technological and organizational origin create a cascade effect when the following phenomena occur: fire, explosion, and toxic release, or any combination of the three. The propagation of danger flows through natural (heat transfer, gas migration), artificial (electrical wiring, ventilation systems), and systems/information networks (spread of a computer virus or errors). In terms of human intervention, panic, stress, and their effects on the organization must be taken into account as they all constitute fields of danger (see Figure 3).

## 4  Cascade Effect Analysis

 Cascade effect analysis (CEA) is a method that has been developed to study and analyze cascade effects on CI. CEA entails five main steps to facilitate the study of CI and the accident sequences generated by their failure (or destruction), taking into account human and organizational factors as well as the interdependencies between critical infrastructures. These five steps are carried out in the following order: A chronological illustration of these steps is given in Section 5, with an example of the domino effect brought about by a liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) storage area, considered an active CI.

## 4.1  Step 1: Setting Up of a Database

 All relevant data (geographical, meteorological, population, etc.) about the CI and its environment are gathered. Any and all information that can help the analyst understand and study the site must be identified. A good way to go about this is to summarize the key information necessary to carry out a QRA of the area. Once the data have been compiled, a database is set up according to the area background and the description of the CI (see Figure 4).

## 4.2  Step 2: Identification of Sources of Danger

 Using the information compiled in the previous step, all internal and/or external potential sources of danger to the CI are identified. This step, which enables the analyst to determine the degree to which each initially identified scenario could escalate, is divided in two substeps: a determination of the hazards associated with the sources of danger, and the selection of an adapted risk analysis methodology to gauge how these sources and their hazards will affect the targets (see Figure 5).

## 4.2  Step 2: Identification of Sources of Danger

 In selecting and identifying the sources of danger, certain criteria have to be taken into account, such as To identify sources of danger, the Method Organized for a Systematic Analysis of Risks, or MOSAR (Perrin et al. 2012), can be used. MOSAR facilitates a risk analysis by breaking down the infrastructure into subsystems and systematically identifying the level to which each subsystem can be a source of danger. To link the sources of danger (source systems) to targets (target systems), the Model of Analysis of Dysfunctional Systems (MADS) (Thivel et al. 2008; Perrin et al. 2012) is highly suited. This method is based on the principle of determining the propagation of the 'low of danger' in a 'danger field' (see Figure 5).

## 4.4.2  Quantitative Risk Assessment Methods

 To study active CI, quantitative risk assessment methods must be used to identify the primary (trigger) events and to quantify the hazards generated by the resulting CI failure. Figure 8 provides a diagram of the events that may occur following the disruption (failure) of active CI.

## 4.4.2  Quantitative Risk Assessment Methods

 To list all the potential accident scenarios involving active CI, an analyst can use the Maximum Credible Accident Scenarios (MCAS) methodology. The aim of this methodology is to list all the 'credible' scenarios, taking into account the three main groups of accident scenario: (1) flammable (fire, explosion); (2) toxic (toxic and/or corrosive releases); and (3) a combination of any two (fire, explosion, and toxic release) (Khan 2002).

## 4.5.1  Scenario Simulation and Consequence Assessment

 The last step consists of an assessment of accident scenarios and associated consequences and the use of that assessment to improve risk reduction strategies. This step is divided in two parts (see Figure 9): An adapted calculation approach based on the preceding steps is proposed. In this approach, the nature of the targets is verified for each initial accident. If necessary, secondary accidents ('active target') are studied with new targets, and so on, and then damage assessments of targets and environment (including people) are carried out. This algorithm is repeated until propagation ceases (see Figure 10).

## 4.5.1.2  Affected Zones

 Some models are able to assess consequences in terms of infrastructure damage and human victims (Abdolhamidzadeh et al. 2010; Kadri et al. 2013). These models are based on the calculation of physical effects with deterministic approaches (mechanical and/or chemical processes, thermodynamics, etc.) - mainly heat loads, pressures, and fragments. These models also have probabilistic aspects, such as the probability of failure as it corresponds to cross-deterministic thresholds, the probability of process propagation, and the probability of damage according to thermomechanical conditions and/or the vulnerability of people. (Defining different affected zones by the number of deaths is proposed.) These probabilities have to be combined according to the identified interdependencies in order to attain the scenario probabilities. Additionally, dispersion and toxic/gas release models can be used to predict the impact that different levels of toxic chemical exposure would have on people.

## 4.5.2  Risk Reduction Strategies

 The second step in the process focuses on improving the strategies to reduce risk. Having weighed the consequences of the damage caused by the accident scenarios (with a cascade effect) using the preceding calculations, an analyst can now identify where there is room for improvement. Ways to enhance mitigation systems can be sought; response and safety/security systems can be analyzed and added to the model when necessary. Intervention plans that have been adapted to the different situations, taking into account emergency management, can be studied (in particularly in terms of logistics, as in Kadri et al. 2012). By including the assessment of the scenario risk levels (probabilities and consequence assessments) and the analysis of the effects of different strategies (mitigation, faster detection, etc.) in the model, risk reduction strategies can be improved.

## 5  Example: Liquefied Petroleum Gas Storage Area

 LPG storage areas contain a great deal of equipment that is susceptible to both external and internal incidents. The physical effects of a fault in an LPG tanker could affect surrounding facilities and equipment, the buildings, the personnel, and the environment. If the targets involved are damaged, they may explode and threaten surrounding facilities, and so on. This accident chain is a domino effect that could have catastrophic consequences for the industrial plant and its environment (particularly people).

## 5  Example: Liquefied Petroleum Gas Storage Area

 In the chemical industry, these storage facilities are a typical example of equipment that would be vulnerable in an earthquake. One clear example of the multiple consequences of damage to LPG storage facilities can be seen in the repercussions of the March 11, 2011, earthquake and tsunami in Japan (see, e.g., Krausmann and Cruz 2013).

## 5.1  Setting Up of a Database (Step 1)

 In the first step in the CEA methodology, the relevant data on the LPG storage area (geographical, population, etc.) are collected. It is useful to summarize the key information necessary to carry out a quantitative risk assessment of this area.

 The aim of this substep is to gather all the relevant data on (1) the geographic location of the LPG storage area and the surrounding infrastructure (passive and/or active), and (2) the population, including its density and distribution around the storage area.

## 5.5  Consequence Assessment and Risk Reduction (Step 5)

 In Step 5 of the CEA methodology, the assumption is that a primary scenario has caused the rupture or explosion of one tank - for example, the TK1 (pressurized tank). This could generate three escalation vectors (physical effects): heat radiation, overpressure waves, and fragments (missiles) and could affect the three nearby storage units (TK2, TK3, and TK4) as well as personnel.

## 5.5  Consequence Assessment and Risk Reduction (Step 5)

 To estimate the risk posed to society, one can disregard the effect on personnel if the probability of death is below a given threshold (e.g., 10 -4).

## 6  Conclusion

 This paper presents a methodology for CI risk assessment in the framework of CEA. This method allows the identification of all sources of danger involving CI, the primary events that may cause CI failure, and the accident sequences generated following that failure (destruction), taking into account human and organizational factors and the interdependencies among CI and/or CI systems (components).

## 6  Conclusion

 This work deals with cascade effects caused by natural events and shows the importance of taking natural risks into account when studying CI, especially in terms of a potential chain of accidents. It then illustrates the suitability of the proposed methodology in assessing the consequences of cascade effects, and discusses the usefulness of such a methodology in establishing risk reduction strategies.

## 6  Conclusion

 Finally, an analysis confirms that the study of natural events in the framework of CEA is necessary given their catastrophic consequences. Hence, much research remains to be done to improve the safety of CI, and much more importance must be placed on the study of natural events and disasters.
